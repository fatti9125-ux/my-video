# Research notes: why you can't tickle yourself

**Core mechanism (used):** when you move, the motor system sends a copy of the motor command (an "efference copy")
to a forward model, thought to involve the cerebellum, which predicts the sensory result of the movement. When the
actual touch matches the prediction, the response to it is attenuated, so self-produced touch feels weaker and less
tickly. External touch can't be predicted the same way, so it isn't dampened.

Sources:
- Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith (1998), "Central cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation", Nature Neuroscience 1(7).
  fMRI: less activity in somatosensory cortex and anterior cingulate for self-produced than external touch; the
  cerebellum responds differently to self-produced touch.
- Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert (1999), "Spatio-temporal prediction modulates the perception of self-produced stimuli",
  Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience 11(5). Adding a delay (100–300 ms) or a rotation between the participant's
  movement and the robot-delivered touch made it feel more tickly, so the effect depends on prediction accuracy.
- Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith (2000), "Why can't you tickle yourself?", NeuroReport 11(11). A review of the above.

Claims in the video, and where they come from:
- "Your brain sends your cerebellum a copy of every move": efference copy and cerebellar forward model (1998, 2000 review).
- "It predicts the touch, and turns it down": sensory attenuation of predicted self-produced touch (1998, 1999).
- "Tickling needs surprise": unpredicted touch is not attenuated; delays that break the prediction restore tickliness (1999).
