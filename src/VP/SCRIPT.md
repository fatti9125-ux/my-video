# Every US Vice President Who Became President Explained in 9 Minutes

One became president after his boss lasted just a month. Another took the oath beside a grieving first lady on an airplane. And one reached the White House without ever winning a presidential or vice-presidential election.

These are all fifteen U.S. vice presidents who became president, how they got there, and what happened after the promotion. One new president’s dad even has to bring out a lamp.

It starts with John Adams, George Washington’s vice president. For eight years, Washington leaned on his cabinet while Adams complained about how insignificant his own job was. Then Washington retired, and Adams won the election of 1796.

There was one awkward detail. Under the original electoral system, the runner-up became vice president. So Adams got Thomas Jefferson, the man who had just run against him. His defeated opponent was now his deputy. Four years later, Jefferson ran against him again.

This time Adams lost, but Jefferson still had somebody to beat. His own running mate, Aaron Burr, had received exactly as many electoral votes. Back then, electors didn’t cast separate votes for president and vice president. The tie went to the House of Representatives, which took thirty-six ballots to choose Jefferson.

America had almost elected the running mate instead of the intended president. Jefferson finally took office in 1801 and later approved the Louisiana Purchase. The Twelfth Amendment changed the electoral system afterward.

After Jefferson, another sitting vice president wouldn’t win the presidency until Martin Van Buren in 1836. Andrew Jackson’s trusted ally looked like a natural successor. Then, just months into his presidency, the Panic of 1837 hit. Banks failed, businesses collapsed, and the country fell into a depression.

Van Buren inherited troubles that had been building before he arrived. Voters still handed him the bill. In 1840, they replaced him with William Henry Harrison. Harrison’s vice president was John Tyler, and Tyler’s promotion came much faster.

Harrison died after thirty-one days in office. Nobody had dealt with a dead president before, and some argued that Tyler was only supposed to exercise presidential powers temporarily. Tyler insisted he had become president outright and took a new oath. His decision established the precedent later vice presidents followed.

His own party soon regretted having him. Tyler vetoed its banking plans, nearly his entire cabinet resigned, and the Whigs expelled him. He kept the presidency and lost the party that put him there.

Millard Fillmore got his turn when Zachary Taylor died in 1850. The country was fighting over slavery and the territories taken from Mexico. Fillmore backed the Compromise of 1850 and signed its separate measures into law.

California entered as a free state, but the package also included a stronger Fugitive Slave Act, helping enslavers seize people who had escaped, even in free states. The supposed settlement deepened anger over slavery. Fillmore finished Taylor’s term, but his party picked someone else for the next election.

Then came Andrew Johnson, whose start as vice president was already a disaster. At his inauguration in March 1865, Johnson had been drinking and delivered a rambling speech while Lincoln watched. About six weeks later, Lincoln was dead, and that same man was president.

Johnson clashed with Congress over Reconstruction and vetoed a civil rights bill. The fight eventually produced the first presidential impeachment. At his Senate trial, the votes for conviction fell one short of the number needed to remove him. His presidency survived by a single vote.

Chester A. Arthur’s promotion came with a deeply uncomfortable association. When James Garfield was shot in 1881, the gunman declared his support for Arthur’s Republican faction and announced that Arthur would be president. Garfield lingered for weeks before dying, leaving Arthur with the office and a deeply uncomfortable association.

Arthur had risen through a system that rewarded allies with government jobs. He surprised critics by signing the Pendleton Act, requiring competitive examinations for certain federal positions. The politician associated with handing out jobs helped change how those jobs were filled.

Theodore Roosevelt’s party bosses had their own staffing solution. New York boss Thomas Platt helped push the troublesome governor toward the vice presidency, getting him out of Albany. In 1901, President McKinley was assassinated, and Roosevelt became president at forty-two.

The plan to move him into a quieter job had put him in the most powerful one. He pursued corporate regulation and conservation, then won the presidency himself in 1904. Being parked in the vice presidency had worked out rather well for Roosevelt.

Calvin Coolidge’s promotion came with considerably less noise. In August 1923, he was visiting his father in Vermont when news arrived that President Warren Harding had died. The house had no electricity.

So his father, a notary public, administered the presidential oath by the light of a kerosene lamp. There’s the lamp. The new president had been sworn in by his dad in the family parlor. Coolidge went on to win the 1924 election.

Harry Truman had only eighty-two days to get used to being vice president. When Franklin Roosevelt died in April 1945, Truman suddenly inherited a world war. Roosevelt had barely included him in major decisions and hadn’t briefed him on the atomic bomb project.

Truman became president before learning the full details of the weapon he would authorize against Japan. He later faced an election many expected him to lose, then defeated Thomas Dewey in 1948. The replacement won a term of his own.

Lyndon B. Johnson also took over after a sudden death, but the transition happened in front of cameras. After Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas in 1963, Johnson took the oath aboard Air Force One. Jacqueline Kennedy stood beside him, with Kennedy’s casket elsewhere on the plane.

Johnson used his congressional experience to help secure the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act, and he won the 1964 election. His escalation of the Vietnam War helped tear apart his presidency. In 1968, he announced he wouldn’t seek another term.

Richard Nixon had spent eight years as Eisenhower’s vice president, then lost the 1960 election to Kennedy. Two years later, he lost the California governor’s race as well. His career seemed to be running backward.

Then he won the presidency in 1968. Eight years as vice president, eight years out of office, then the top job. Watergate eventually destroyed that comeback. In 1974, Nixon became the first U.S. president to resign.

Which brings us to Gerald Ford. Nixon’s original vice president, Spiro Agnew, had already resigned. Nixon nominated Ford to replace him, and Congress confirmed him. Eight months later, Nixon resigned too.

Ford became president without winning an election for either national office. A month afterward, he pardoned Nixon, igniting another political firestorm. He tried to move the country past Watergate, but the pardon followed him into his losing campaign against Jimmy Carter.

George H.W. Bush took a more familiar route. After eight years as Ronald Reagan’s vice president, he won the 1988 election. He was the first sitting vice president to win since Van Buren, a gap of 152 years.

Bush led the coalition that drove Iraqi forces out of Kuwait. At home, he agreed to a budget deal that raised taxes after campaigning against new ones. His foreign-policy success didn’t secure another term. Bill Clinton defeated him in 1992.

Joe Biden needed three presidential campaigns. His bids for the 1988 and 2008 nominations failed, but Barack Obama chose him as his running mate. Biden served eight years as vice president, spent four years out of office, then defeated Donald Trump in 2020.

He took office at seventy-eight, setting the age record at the time. His presidency included a bipartisan infrastructure law, and he ended his reelection campaign in 2024. Decades after his first attempt, he’d served a full term.

That makes fifteen. Eight first became president because a president died. Ford got there through a resignation. Six reached it by winning an election, including Nixon and Biden after time out of office.

Their first days ranged from a family parlor to an airplane carrying a murdered president home. And the precedent behind the emergency promotions goes back to Tyler, the man whose own party threw him out while he kept the White House.

Of course, plenty of vice presidents spent years waiting for that promotion and never got it. Their attempts produced a whole different collection of political disasters.
